The travel-time midpoint loss on a 9-DOF mobile manipulator

The Gibson-house rectangle robot now carries a six-joint arm (a UR5e at one fifth of its size): 9 degrees of freedom, planned through three scanned houses with an ntrl-demo travel-time field. Adding the recursive travel-time midpoint loss at epoch 2,000 changes the share of solved queries by +4.1 points in Allensville, +12.7 points in Lakeville, +13.9 points in Klickitat against the paired control (the same resumed training without the term).

Answers

Does the midpoint loss help on the 9-DOF task?
Allensville: midpoint 89.4 %, control 85.4 %, baseline 83.7 % (3 training seeds); Lakeville: midpoint 63.4 %, control 50.8 %, baseline 50.1 % (3 training seeds); Klickitat: midpoint 71.3 %, control 57.4 %, baseline 55.7 % (3 training seeds). It helps in every training seed in Lakeville (+13.3, +14.6, +10.0 points against the control) and Klickitat (+13.8, +11.8, +16.1 points against the control), the houses where the baseline field sees through walls; in Allensville, where the baseline already plans well, it helps in 2 of 3 seeds (+7.1, +7.8, -2.6; +4.1 pooled).
What does it fix?
Stalls: runs that never arrive, control → midpoint, Allensville 6.7 → 0.6 %; Lakeville 21.5 → 11.4 %; Klickitat 16.9 → 8.7 %. Many baseline stalls are short queries where only the arm has to move (local minima of the field in joint space). And the short far field: queries whose learned T(start, goal) lies below the base's physical lower bound, Allensville 29 → 2 %; Lakeville 46 → 7 %; Klickitat 39 → 7 %.
What does it leave?
Runs that drive through walls. Allensville: 8.0 → 10.0 % of runs, median penetration 0.09 → 0.09 cells; Lakeville: 27.8 → 25.2 % of runs, median penetration 0.37 → 0.36 cells; Klickitat: 25.7 → 20.0 % of runs, median penetration 0.41 → 0.19 cells. In Lakeville they stay deep crossings of walls the field does not see. On the longest queries (32 cells and more) the change against the control is Allensville -2 points; Lakeville +5 points; Klickitat +15 points. In Allensville the contacts stay shallow clips of corners (seed 87358 clipped one corner of the central block on most of its failed long queries).
Why some walls and not others?
The loss learns only from the drives the current field can make. A drive that heads into an unseen wall is cut at the wall or stalls in front of it, so no training pair ever holds the detour around that wall. Training drives that reach their goal, first → last refresh: Allensville 82 → 99 %; Lakeville 44 → 70 %; Klickitat 55 → 77 %. Where the drives learn to get around a wall, the field learns it (Klickitat's contacts get fewer and shallower); where a third of the drives still stall, deep crossings stay (Lakeville). This is the same limit the lift + midpoint study found in 2-D (on-policy data).
Does the arm matter?
Yes. Of the runs that arrive through something, the arm touches first in Allensville 39 %; Lakeville 30 %; Klickitat 39 % (baseline, planning seed 0), every time with the base itself clear of the wall. The baseline field does not fold the arm near walls: the share of tucked waypoints within half a cell of a wall minus the share in the open is -0.03, -0.02, -0.01 (Allensville, Lakeville, Klickitat). With the midpoint loss it is +0.13, +0.02, -0.02 (control +0.06, -0.02, -0.05): the arm learns to fold near walls only where the drives succeed almost always (Allensville).
How much harder is it than the base alone?
The baseline on the bare SE(2) rectangle (the lift-scale study's ntrl-demo runs, same houses and bins, their own queries) against the 9-DOF baseline: Allensville 90.3 → 83.7 %; Lakeville 69.1 → 50.1 %; Klickitat 71.2 → 55.7 %. The arm costs most in the two large houses, where far more of the baseline's runs drive through walls.
What does it cost?
A baseline field trains in 7–8 minutes. The midpoint run drives 8,192 pairs every 100 epochs and that dominates its cost (RTX 3090: 178–269 s per refresh, 1.6–2.4 h per run; RTX 5070: 180–277 s per refresh, 1.6–2.4 h per run).

The robot

The base is the rectangle every Gibson-house study here uses: 0.5 × 0.2 cells, 12.5 × 5 cm at 0.25 m per cell, the size that reaches every room of Allensville, Lakeville and Klickitat. A wheeled base on the floor moves in x, y and heading, so with the arm the robot has 9 degrees of freedom. The arm is the repository's UR5e (its URDF and its 28 collision spheres) at one fifth of its size, mounted on the centre of the base's top. It reaches up to 0.79 cells from the mount, so reaching sideways it sticks out more than half a cell past the base. Folded straight up, every sphere sits inside the base's footprint, so the tucked robot fits wherever the rectangle fits.

Walls are the houses' floor plans extruded upward, so they stop the arm as well as the base. The arm can also hit the floor, the base and itself (cuRobo's UR5e self-collision pairs). The speed is ntrl-demo's label of the robot's clearance, with the arm's internal distances counted at a 3.5 times smaller margin. The arm's own parts sit closer than the wall margin in every pose, so without that the robot would be slow everywhere.

Queries solved

Every field plans the same certified queries with ntrl-demo's MPPI, three planning seeds each. A run counts if it arrives and no configuration on its path touches a wall, the floor, the base or the arm itself. The midpoint run and the control both continue the baseline's own epoch-2,000 network, so the three fields of a training seed share their first 2,000 epochs.

How the runs end

The planner appends the goal to every path, so a run that stalls also ends with a jump to the goal. Runs are therefore split by arrival: arrived cleanly, arrived through something (the part touched first is recorded), or never arrived.

Which part touches first

For every run that arrived through something, the first configuration that touches anything is found on the dense path, and the part that touches is recorded: the arm against a wall, the base against a wall, or the arm against the floor, the base or itself.

How deep the first contact goes separates two failures. In the large houses the baseline's contacts are deep: the path crosses a wall the field does not see. In Allensville they are shallow clips of corners. Allensville: control 57 contacts (median depth 0.09 cells, 31 under 0.1 cells), midpoint 72 (0.09 cells, 42 shallow); Lakeville: control 232 contacts (median depth 0.37 cells, 24 under 0.1 cells), midpoint 209 (0.36 cells, 33 shallow); Klickitat: control 218 contacts (median depth 0.41 cells, 16 under 0.1 cells), midpoint 168 (0.19 cells, 33 shallow).

What the loss does to the field

No 9-D ground truth exists, but the optimum is bracketed. The bare rectangle's time between the two base poses, T*_SE2, is a lower bound: the 9-D robot's clearance never exceeds the rectangle's and its metric never charges less. The reference path (tuck, drive the SE(2) optimum, untuck) is an upper bound. A field whose T(start, goal) falls below T*_SE2 promises a trip that no path can make, which is what a field that does not see a wall does.

The houses

The houses are the floor plans of the earlier house studies (cuts of the scanned mesh 0.4–1.6 m above the floor, 0.25 m per cell). Starts are random configurations; goals are the earlier studies' 48 truth goal poses with random arms.

How it was run

  • Configuration: q = (x, y, φ, ψ₁…ψ₆). x, y span the house box. Heading φ = 0.25 θ / E, as in the house studies. Joint ψⱼ = ρⱼ qⱼ / E with ρⱼ = the joint's largest lever arm (0.76, 0.76, 0.42, 0.10, 0.06, 0.05 cells), so turning a joint costs like moving the farthest sphere it carries.
  • Training data: ntrl-demo's pair recipe in the 9-D box, 500,000 pairs. In Allensville, 34 % of the labels come from the base against a wall, 23 % from the arm against a wall and 43 % from the arm's internal constraints.
  • Queries: Allensville 240 (43 cells across), Lakeville 280 (83 cells across), Klickitat 280 (96 cells across). Each is certified by a collision-free reference path: tuck, drive the SE(2) optimum, untuck.
  • Baseline: ntrl-demo's models/metric with dim 9, 5,000 epochs, its own loop (checked identical to the package's Model.train() over the first epochs), training seeds 87358, 1, 2.
  • Midpoint: the baseline's epoch-2,000 network and optimizer, then 3,000 epochs with jeffrey/midpoint_dp's main setting. Every 100 epochs, 8,192 safe MPPI drives between random free configurations, cut at the first contact or stall. Each drive is split at travel-time midpoints down to one cell of travel time, with the exact speed and min leaves; tree and sub-path nodes; |log T − log y|, weight ramped to 1 over 500 epochs.
  • Control: the same resumed loop without the term. A paired design, because two runs of one seed already differ by up to 12 points across the two GPUs; on one GPU a run repeats exactly.
  • Planner: ntrl-demo's MPPI (50 samples, horizon 5, the house studies' step scaling) over all nine coordinates, with angles clamped to the box. Budget is 3 × the reference path's length. Planning seeds 0, 1, 2; exact scoring every 0.005 cells per coordinate.

Caveats

  • Walls are prisms from the floor plan: furniture lower than 0.4 m is not in the plan, and the arm cannot reach over anything.
  • The arm's internal distances use a 0.05-cell margin and the walls 0.175 cells. That is a modelling choice: the UR5e's own links sit closer than the wall margin in every pose.
  • No 9-D optimum is known; times are bracketed between the bare rectangle's optimum and the reference path.
  • The SE(2) comparison uses the lift-scale study's own queries (same houses and distance bins), not these.
  • Runs of one training seed are paired; the three fields of a seed still ran on whichever GPU was free.
