Where to put two arms in front of the cupboard

3-D view of every placement and of the ten configurations: arm placement viewer.

Moving the two arms back makes the task easier, as far back as they can still reach into the cupboard. The best placement tested is 1.00 m from the cupboard front (bases ±0.20 m from the centre plane, 0.80 m above the floor). There the repository’s lift field solves 97 % of the certified queries and 94 % of the zone-to-another-zone queries, when the planner walks from both ends at once; the ntrl-demo field solves 8 %. At the earlier study’s 0.25 m the same field and ntrl-demo’s own planner solved 36 %. The price is depth: at 1.00 m the hands only work the front 13 cm of the 53 cm deep shelves.

The price
At 1.00 m the grasp point is 6 cm inside the cupboard in the median zone → zone query and never more than 13 cm (shelves: 53 cm deep). What made the difference
The planner first: the same lift field goes from 36 % to 64 % at 0.25 m and from 54 % to 95 % at 1.00 m when it is used from both ends. The field is symmetric, yet its planner leaves a zone far more reliably than it enters one. Then the distance, and part of that gain is that far placements only have shallow queries: 85 % at 0.55 m, 83 % at 0.70 m, 86 % at 0.78 m, 88 % at 0.85 m, 88 % at 0.92 m, 92 % at 1.00 m of the queries 7 to 13 cm inside the cupboard are solved.

Distance from the cupboard

The cupboard, the test recipe and the training recipe stay fixed; only the bases move. Each placement gets its own certified test queries (six kinds × 50), its own training pairs and its own two fields. Both learned fields gain from distance. So do the two controls that use no learned field, which says the task itself gets easier: the planner on plain joint distance solves 18 % at 0.55 m, 19 % at 0.70 m, 20 % at 0.78 m, 23 % at 0.85 m, 20 % at 0.92 m, 24 % at 1.00 m.

Which queries get easier

With ntrl-demo’s planner (left) distance helps the queries that end in free space and does nothing for those that end inside the cupboard. At 1.00 m, bases ±0.30 m, 0.60 m high the lift field solves 87 % of zone → free and 7 % of free → zone, although T(a, b) = T(b, a). Run from the goal to the start, the same planner gives the mirror image (80 % and 10 %). So the field knows the way out of a zone and the planner loses the last stretch on the way in. The two-ended planner (right) only ever asks for the way out.

The two-ended planner

bidir.plan_bidir: one head starts at the start, one at the goal. In every iteration each head takes one ordinary MPPI step of ntrl-demo’s planner towards the other head’s current position. They stop when they meet; the path is the first head’s path followed by the second one’s reversed. Same field, same step, same 50 samples, no collision checker. At 1.00 m, bases ±0.30 m, 0.60 m high: ntrl-demo’s planner, 600 steps 54 %; two-ended, 300 steps per end (the same 600 moves) 77 %; two-ended, 600 per end 85 %; 1,200 per end 91 %; 2,400 per end 95 %; 4,800 per end 96 %. More steps do not rescue the one-ended planner: 55 % at 2,400 steps, 55 % at 4,800 steps. More samples per step do not help either (84 % with 200 samples at 600 steps per end). A third variant, both ends walking to a retracted pose chosen by the field, was worse (49 % at 0.85 m against 79 % two-ended).

Using the two 48 GB cards

The cards were limited by compute and then by memory, in that order. A single training process leaves a 4090 mostly idle (it waits on Python), so the scheduler (queue/sched.py) runs many registered jobs per card: 12 training-like processes together did 446 steps per second against 103 for one, on a card that was generating test suites at the same time. With 25 to 30 jobs in flight both cards sat at 40 to 47 GB, and three jobs died from out-of-memory before the scheduler began reserving each job’s peak VRAM. CUDA MPS made it worse in this mix (6 processes: 36 steps per second each with MPS, 67 without), so it is off. Bigger batches where they pay: the pair sampler draws 2^20 candidate configurations at a time (a prepare run takes 0.6 to 5 minutes; 8 minutes in the earlier study), the suite generator batches every stratum’s RRT queries together (about 1,400 per round). The slow part was certifying test queries: RRT-Connect at the scorer’s 0.001 rad resolution is bound by memory bandwidth, not by batch size.

Saved models

Everything is under jeffrey/arm_placement/models/: one folder per placement with the weights of every finished field (lift_s87358.pt, ntrl_s87358.pt, second seeds where trained), the scene files the lift needs, and the test queries; models/best/ holds the chosen placement’s full checkpoints; models/index.json lists registered run ids and solved counts. This machine has no persistent volume and the folder is not committed: copy it off before the instance is recycled.

Setup and limits

  • Two models: my reading of the request, not confirmed: ntrl-demo (cloned from github.com/ruiqini/ntrl-demo, commit 5ed065f, unmodified) and the repository’s LBS field in its best two-arm setting from jeffrey/lbs_arm (travel-time lift, half Fourier frequency, no Bellman or segment term). 5,000 epochs, training seeds 87358 and 87359.
  • Scene: the measured cupboard and both UR5e with grippers, as in jeffrey/real_world. A run is solved when every configuration of the path, checked every 0.001 rad per joint, keeps 5 mm from the cupboard, floor and pedestals, 5 mm between the arms and 2 mm self-clearance.
  • Test queries: per placement, six kinds × 50, each certified by a witness path (straight line, RRT-Connect in 12 joints, or one arm at a time). The witness planner ran 3,000 iterations per stage instead of the earlier study’s 10,000, so these sets lean somewhat more towards queries a sampling planner solves quickly. The 0.25 m point uses the earlier study’s frozen 600 queries. Its suite with this study’s recipe, 0.40 m, and the 0.80 m height at 0.55 and 0.70 m were stopped: their suites took over two hours each.
  • Different queries at every placement. A placement changes which queries exist, so the comparison across distances is between tasks defined the same way, not between identical queries.
  • Reproduction check: on the earlier study’s placement and frozen suite the lift field solves 645 of 1,800 (earlier: 673 and 639 on two seeds), ntrl-demo 15.
  • Controls on the lift at 1.00 m, bases ±0.30 m, 0.60 m high: Fourier frequency 0.25 instead of 0.5: 54 % with ntrl-demo’s planner, 96 % two-ended (0.5: 54 % and 95 %). Frequency 1.0: 22 % with ntrl-demo’s planner. The full LBS loss (Bellman and segment terms) took 8 seconds per epoch next to the other jobs and was stopped at epoch 1,500 of 5,000; its validation counts there were 141, 151, 150 of 300 at epochs 500, 1,000, 1,500 against 163, 163, 158 for the lift alone.
  • Training length: the lift field’s validation count is flat from epoch 500 on (163 of 300 at epoch 500, 165 at epoch 5,000, at 1.00 m). The 5,000 epochs were kept so that every run matches the earlier study.
  • Planner steps: a step is 0.0615 cells, about 0.03 rad of joint motion. 2,400 steps per end is eight times the moves of the default budget; planning 300 queries that way took 80 seconds on a shared card.
