 
 2026-11-11 Travel-Time Lift 
lift_warp study · follow-up to the max-min warp page · every number from registered runs and read-only forensics on their checkpoints 
Why raising the slowest coordinate speed did not help, and the version of the idea that does 
The max-min map raises the slowest coordinate speed by 1.11–1.50 where the wall band needs a factor of 83, and pays for it with free space; the trained networks build no more of the wall layer than without a map. The idea works once the minimum is taken along the gradient and the room for the band comes from a third input coordinate, h(x) = the travel time out of the band along the normal: ntrl-demo then satisfies the eikonal equation in every speed bin (S × slope 0.98–1.02) within about 100 epochs, with free space untouched. That exposes a second problem the smeared wall layer had been hiding: the eikonal loss accepts fields that pass straight through obstacles, and the corner trap and the stalls of the max-min page are the same family. The true field is the largest solution, and a push on pairs whose straight segment is blocked selects it. Lift plus push, disks / floor plan / plus maze / gap maze: 100.0 / 94.2 / 64.9 / 91.5 % of queries solved and 99.0 / 82.1 / 86.3 / 92.5 % of travel times within 10 % (plain ntrl-demo 96.6 / 61.1 / 68.5 / 53.1 % and 90.4 / 22.0 / 9.7 / 43.6 %). In queries solved that is better on disks (+3 points), floor plan (+33 points), gap maze (+38 points); worse on plus maze (-4 points). The plus maze needs one more thing the lift cannot give, room between the two sides of a thin wall. With a wall-magnifying planar map in front of the lift: 100.0 / 98.3 / 99.8 / 99.3 % solved and 98.9 / 81.9 / 83.3 / 92.0 % within 10 %. 
Answers 
1. Why did raising the minimum speed not make the field easier to learn? 
 it could not The network is limited by how fast its slope can change per unit of its input, and the max-min map gives the wall band no more input length. R is 1.11–1.50 where the band’s slope spans a factor of 83, and the room for the larger obstacles comes out of free space (5 % of it compressed to 0.05–0.41 or less). The trained networks built the same wall layer as without a map: 0.09 of the required slope against 0.10 on the plus maze. 
2. Is the idea wrong? 
The reasoning holds; the minimum was taken over the wrong set and paid for in the wrong place. The gradient of a far field in the band points along the wall normal, and that is the only direction that needs speed. A map of the plane onto itself has no room to give it. 
3. What is the adapted version? 
 yes A lift instead of a warp: the network’s input is (x, h(x)), with h the travel time from x out of the speed band along the normal, a closed-form function of the clearance. Coordinate speed along normals is 1 everywhere, free space is untouched, and the minimum over gradient directions is 59 times the floor (the max-min value R stays exactly 1). 
4. Does the network then learn the sharp part? 
 yes Exactly. S × |∇T| is 0.98–1.02 in every speed bin on every scene and seed, reached within about 100 epochs (plain ntrl-demo: 0.08–0.26 in the innermost band after 5,000). Travel times within 10 %, disks / floor plan / plus maze / gap maze: 96.6 / 61.8 / 74.5 / 83.9 % against 90.4 / 22.0 / 9.7 / 43.6 % for plain ntrl-demo. 
5. Does it plan better? 
Not by itself. Queries solved with the lift alone: 95.8 / 65.0 / 57.9 / 56.4 % (plain 96.6 / 61.1 / 68.5 / 53.1 %). With the wall layer exact, a second problem becomes visible: the eikonal loss accepts fields that go straight through obstacles, and plain ntrl-demo only avoided them as a side effect of its smeared wall layer. With the largest-solution push added: 100.0 / 94.2 / 64.9 / 91.5 % solved, 99.0 / 82.1 / 86.3 / 92.5 % within 10 %. Against plain ntrl-demo that is, in queries solved, better on disks (+3 points), floor plan (+33 points), gap maze (+38 points); worse on plus maze (-4 points); in travel times within 10 %, better on disks (+9 points), floor plan (+60 points), plus maze (+77 points), gap maze (+49 points). 
6. And the plus maze, where lift and push stop short? 
 yes The lift cannot tell the two sides of a thin wall apart: they have the same clearance, so the same height. A planar map that magnifies wall interiors, placed in front of the lift, can. Map + lift + push, disks / floor plan / plus maze / gap maze: 100.0 / 98.3 / 99.8 / 99.3 % solved and 98.9 / 81.9 / 83.3 / 92.0 % within 10 % (3 / 3 / 3 / 3 seeds). The map is the wall-magnifying kind from the earlier pages; alone it planned 78.1 / 92.3 / 94.9 / 69.0 % with 72.2 / 70.2 / 11.7 / 63.2 % within 10 %. On a 30 × 30 plus maze no setting was chosen on: 97.3 % solved against 70.2 % without the map (one seed). So a diffeomorphism has a job after all, and it is giving obstacles room, not raising the slowest speed (Part 3). 
7. Is it generalizable? 
The lift needs the clearance and the speed profile, nothing else: no mesh, no optimization, no solver, any dimension. The push needs a collision check of straight segments. The wall-magnifying map is the part that does not generalize for free: it comes from a lattice optimizer that runs on any 2-D scene in minutes but has no 3-D version, and a closed-form substitute recovers only part of its effect (Part 3). One lift-and-push setting was used on all scenes, including two larger mazes it was not chosen on: 30 × 30 plus maze 70.2 % solved and 86.0 % within 10 % against 66.7 % and 26.8 % for plain ntrl-demo; gap maze ×4 75.1 % solved and 96.7 % within 10 % against 36.8 % and 51.8 % for plain ntrl-demo (one seed each). It has not been run in 3-D or in a robot’s configuration space, where the clearance gradient is not a unit vector and the equalization is approximate. 
Part 1 · Why raising the minimum did not help 
With y = φ(x) the learned field is T(x 0 , x 1 ) = H(φ(x 0 ), φ(x 1 )), and the slope the network must build in its own input coordinates is one over the coordinate speed. The reasoning behind the max-min map is sound up to that point. Three things break it between there and the learner. 
1. The network is limited by how fast its slope can change, and the map does not change that 
ntrl-demo’s speed falls from 1 to the floor 0.012 inside a band 0.175 cells wide next to every obstacle, so the field’s slope along the wall normal must rise from 1 to 83 within that band. The earlier chord_warp study measured what the network does there: the slope it reaches is proportional to the width of the band in its input coordinates (slope − 1 ≈ 170–390 divided by the scene size in cells), not capped at a value. What it lacks is input length over which to turn the slope up. The chart shows that length for four coordinate systems on the plus maze. 
Without a map the slope goes from 83 to 1 in 0.175 cells. The max-min map leaves that profile where it was: the floor is 1.11–1.50 times higher (R on the four scenes), so the innermost band asks 22–30 instead of 34, and the band is no longer than before along the normal (0.15–0.24 cells of input against 0.157). The objective is met by enlarging the obstacle floor and interiors by R in every direction; a band point with speed above the new minimum is not asked to stretch at all, and is free to shrink. 
The room for the larger obstacles comes out of free space. Half of the free points are compressed to 0.18–0.82 of their size in one direction and 5 % of them to 0.05–0.41 or less, with anisotropy up to 39 (95th percentile). Free space asked a slope of exactly 1 before; now a twentieth of it asks 2.4–21 or more. Measured over the directions a field’s gradient can take, the ratio between the fastest and the slowest coordinate speed goes from 72 without a map to 109–179 with the max-min map: the field became more uneven, not less. The companion page on speeds along optimal paths shows the same thing path by path. 
2. The networks did not build more of the wall layer 
The direct test: S × |∇T| of each trained network at held-out band points, by speed. It is 1 where the eikonal equation holds and below 1 where the network’s slope falls short. 
On the plus maze plain ntrl-demo builds 0.10 of the required slope in the innermost band and the max-min field 0.09; on the floor plan 0.08 and 0.09. Where R is largest (gap maze, 1.49) the fit moves from 0.14 to 0.24, and that is the one scene where max-min planned better (+18 points). So the gain exists and has the right sign, but its size is set by R, and R is 1.1–1.5 where the band needs a factor of 83. 
3. The objective ranks coordinate systems backwards 
The max-min objective takes the minimum over every direction at every point. A far field’s gradient inside the band is close to the wall normal (Snell’s law: its tangential slowness is at most 1 while the normal one is up to 83), so the tangential direction in the band, where the minimum sits, is never used. Left: R as the objective defines it, evaluated on the training points. Right: the same minimum over the gradient’s own directions. 
By R the max-min map is the best of the four (1.11–1.50), the planar band map of chord_warp is worse than no map (0.34–0.67: it squeezes the band tangentially to stretch it across), and the lift introduced below is exactly 1.00. The learned wall layer orders them the other way round. 
Why a map of the plane onto itself cannot do better 
Evening out the slope along the normal means stretching the band by 1 / S across the wall. The trained band (0.0175–0.175 cells) then needs ∫ dd / S = 0.75 cells instead of 0.16, and 3.7 cells with the floor layer, on each side of every wall. In the plus maze a corridor is 0.5 cells wide, 0.35 of it band; equalized at free-space scale it would need 1.65 cells. The only material that is not trained on is the inside of the walls, 0.47 cells per corridor. A planar self-map therefore pays with free space (max-min, diffusion) or stops at a partial stretch (the band map reaches ×4–5 in the innermost band by emptying the walls). Max-min is further from it than the others because it requires the obstacles to grow as well. 
Part 2 · The adapted idea: take the minimum along the gradient, and take the room from a third coordinate 
Two changes to the max-min idea follow from Part 1. First, the minimum is taken over the directions the gradient takes, which in the band means the cone around the wall normal. Second, the map does not have to send the plane onto itself. The network’s input can be a surface in three dimensions, and then the length the band needs along the normal can go upward instead of sideways. 
φ(x) = ( x , h(x) ) h(x) = G(d(x)) G(d) = ∫_d^margin √( 1 / S(d')² − 1 ) dd' h = 0 in free space
along the wall normal: surface length = √(1 + G'²) dd = dd / S → coordinate speed 1 everywhere
any far-field gradient: speed = 1 / √(1 + p² (1 − S²)) ∈ [0.707, 1] (p ≤ 1 its tangential slowness)
free space: φ is the identity, nothing is compressed
all directions (max-min): R = 1.000 
A patch of the plus maze. Left: the speed the eikonal loss sees (the band is the thin rim around each wall). Middle: the third coordinate h, the travel time from the point out of the band along the normal (0 in free space, 0.69 cells at the inner edge of the trained band, 3.6 cells at the depth the training pairs reach). Right: the surface the network takes as input. Free space is flat and untouched; every corridor has become a canyon whose walls are as long, in input coordinates, as the time it takes to climb them. 
h is a one-dimensional integral of the speed profile, evaluated at the clearance d(x) that the speed is already computed from. There is no optimization, no mesh and no solver, and nothing in it is specific to two dimensions. ntrl-demo is unchanged except that its Fourier features take three inputs instead of two; every term of its loss is still differentiated with respect to x through the chain rule (checked to 6 × 10⁻⁷). 
In the terms of Part 1: along normals the requested slope is 1 in every speed bin, the band has 0.72 cells of input length instead of 0.16 (2.2 with the floor layer), free space asks exactly what it asked before, and the minimum speed over gradient directions is 59 times the floor. By the max-min objective itself the lift does nothing (R = 1.000): the tangential direction in the band is as slow as ever. No far field uses it. 
What it does: the wall layer is learned exactly, and within about a hundred epochs 
The lifted fields are the fourth line in the four charts of Part 1: S × |∇T| is between 0.98 and 1.02 in every speed bin on every scene (plain ntrl-demo: 0.08–0.26 in the innermost band). It also gets there fast. Below, the innermost-band fit of one seed scored at ntrl-demo’s own early checkpoints. 
With the wall layer right, travel times along free lines of sight are right too. On random pairs whose true path is within 5 % of the straight line, the share of learned times within 10 % of the truth is 99 % with the lift against 93 % for plain ntrl-demo (disks; median learned / true 0.99 against 1.02), 95 % with the lift against 25 % for plain ntrl-demo (floor plan; median learned / true 1.01 against 1.20), 92 % with the lift against 7 % for plain ntrl-demo (plus maze; median learned / true 1.03 against 1.19), 97 % with the lift against 46 % for plain ntrl-demo (gap maze; median learned / true 1.02 against 1.11). Plain ntrl-demo cannot draw a thin wall layer and spreads it into the free space next to every wall, which is where its 10–20 % overestimate on the mazes comes from.

Input length alone lets the network build most or all of the wall layer, so that is what the layer needs. The travel-time profile is the version with no slope change left: its fit is exact on every scene and seed, and on the open scene its travel times are right where the clearance coordinate’s are 9 % short. On the floor plan the clearance coordinate plans better than the lift alone; its wall layer is less exact there (one seed 0.63, with slopes of 1.2–1.3 spilling into free space), which is the regime in which plain ntrl-demo keeps its detours. 
How much of the lift is needed? In a run where the third coordinate was faded in between epochs 300 and 1,500 (plus maze), the innermost-band fit went from 0.04 at epoch 300 to 1.04 at epoch 400, when the coordinate was at 8 % of its full size. At this scene size a twelfth of the lift already gives the band enough input length; the full lift is the size at which the answer no longer depends on the scene’s scale. 
The closed problem becomes learnable 
ntrl-demo never samples inside an obstacle, and chord_warp showed that closing the interiors at the true floor speed breaks plain ntrl-demo: it is asked for slope 83 over a third of its first ends and inflates every travel time. What it exposes: the eikonal loss does not decide what happens across an obstacle 
On the gap maze the lifted fields have an exact wall layer, 83.9 % of random pairs within 10 % (plain ntrl-demo 43.6 %), and they plan no better than plain ntrl-demo: 56.4 % against 53.1 %, 43.4 % of the queries end in a stall. The reason is a property of the training problem that the smeared wall layer of plain ntrl-demo had been hiding. 
ntrl-demo's loss at a pair (x0, x1): S(x0) |∇T| = 1 at x0 and S(x1) |∇T| = 1 at x1
(local, and blind to the sign of the gradient)
each of these satisfies it almost everywhere:
 T = min( true time , straight distance + barrier heights + c ) c ≥ 0 a crossing cost per wall
 "see-through": for c = 0 an obstacle is a bump, not a wall
 T = min( true time , c0 + |x0 − p| ) p any point, c0 any head start
 a spurious sink at p: the corner trap, the stalls
all of them are ≤ the true travel time: the largest function whose slope nowhere exceeds 1 / S. 
A detour index makes this measurable: ρ = (T net − D) / (T* − D) on random pairs whose true path is at least 30 % longer than the straight distance D. ρ = 0 means the field is the straight distance there, ρ = 1 the truth. 
 The lifted field starts on the see-through solution and leaves it slowly. Gap maze: ρ = 0.01–0.08 up to epoch 1,000 (98–99 % of detour pairs seen through), 0.65 at epoch 5,000. Along lines of sight it is right from the first hundred epochs. 
 Plain ntrl-demo’s detours are a side effect of its smeared wall layer. In the faded-in run the plain phase reaches ρ = 0.69 by epoch 300; at epoch 400, with the third coordinate at 8 % of its size, ρ is 0.10. Faded in later (from epoch 1,000): ρ 1.36 at epoch 1,000, 0.21 at epoch 1,200. A network that cannot build a thin barrier raises the slope in both bands of a wall by letting its embedding run monotonically across the wall, which separates the two sides as a by-product (with travel times 10–20 % long). Once the barrier can be drawn exactly that pressure is gone, and nothing in the loss keeps the two sides apart. 
 The stalls are places where the learned time is too short. Gap maze: 125–133 of 300 plans stall per seed, at points where the learned time is 0.59 of the truth (median). Disks: two seeds plan 100 %; the third stalls on 38 queries, all 38 of them in the box corner, in open space, where the learned time is 0.79 of the truth. That is the corner trap of the max-min page, here with free space untouched by any map: it does not come from compressing the corner. 
So the two false minima of the max-min page and the see-through field are one thing: a local minimum of T that is not the goal, which the loss cannot see because it checks slopes, not where they lead. Each of them makes the field smaller than the true one. 
Selecting the largest solution 
The true travel time is the largest member of that family, so the selection rule is to push T up wherever the slope constraint allows it. This is how quasimetric reinforcement learning and maximum-variance unfolding recover geodesic distances from local constraints. The pairs a see-through field gets wrong can be found without a solver: their straight segment crosses an obstacle (a collision check on 48 points of the segment). 
loss = β [ ntrl-demo's loss
 − λ(epoch) · mean of T over pairs whose straight segment crosses an obstacle and whose T is below 3 box lengths
 + μ · mean of relu(√(S |∇T|) − 1)² at both ends ]
first term: push the too-short pairs up (bounded, so pairs that no path connects stop being pushed).
second term: keep the slope from exceeding 1 / S.
λ: 0 at epoch 200, 1e-2 from 600 to 2,000, 3e-3 from 3,000 on. μ = 0.1.
ntrl-demo's own loss value (its β and its epoch-repeat rule) is left as it is. 
Gap maze, same seed: at epoch 1,000 the detour index is 0.08 without the push and 0.78 with a constant push of λ = 3e-3, which reaches 0.96 by epoch 2,000 with line-of-sight times still 1.03 of the truth. Three times the push gets there by epoch 600 and then overshoots: index 1.53, line-of-sight times 1.10. The plus maze has longer detours and needs the stronger push to move at all in the first 1,500 epochs. Hence the schedule: strong until the detours are there, then relaxed. 
The push has to be bounded: the floor plan 
On the floor plan the push first works and then destroys the field. At epoch 1,000 the 5 fields trained with it solve 90–97 % of the queries with 49–86 % of travel times within 10 % (plain ntrl-demo: 61.1 % and 22.0 % at its final epoch). At epoch 5,000 the 6 that have finished solve 48 % with 2 % within 10 %: the whole field has shrunk (median learned / true 0.46). The cause is in the scene: 44 % of its free points lie outside the house and no path connects them to the inside, so 41 % of the training pairs have a true travel time of infinity. The push does what it should with them and never stops: their learned time is 1.2 times the straight distance at epoch 400, 6 times at 600 and 42 times at 800, 269 cells, which is as far apart as the network can put two points. Its embedding is normalized, so once inside and outside take all of its range the real distances shrink: learned / true on connected pairs 0.98 at epoch 1,000, 0.75 at 1,500, 0.69 at 2,000. 
The fix is a bound: a pair is pushed only while its learned time is below three box lengths. The largest true travel time is 1.5–1.8 box lengths on every scene here, so the bound is inactive on connected pairs and stops the others. With the bound the unconnected pairs stay at 3.5–5.2 box lengths through training instead of 24. With it the floor plan gives 94.2 % solved and 82.1 % within 10 % at the final epoch (3 seeds). Where free space is connected the bound never acts: trained with it, one seed per scene gives disks 100.0 % / 99.0 %; plus maze 68.7 % / 88.2 %; gap maze 91.7 % / 89.9 %, identical to the run of the same seed without the bound. The recipe’s numbers on this page are the bounded push on the floor plan and, on the other scenes, the three seeds trained before the bound was added.

Part 3 · What the lift cannot do: give a thin wall two sides 
The height h depends on the clearance alone, so two points that face each other across a wall get the same height. On the plus maze such points are 0.66 cells apart in the plane (wall 0.5 cells), and the travel times to a goal behind the wall differ between them by the whole detour (a median of 14 cells in the longest class of detours). The lift gives the network no input length for that difference. Under the strong push the network builds it anyway; once the push is relaxed it lets the long detours go. Giving the wall room is a job for the position coordinates, and a planar map can do it. 
A wall-magnifying planar map in front of the lift 
φ(x) = ( ψ(x) , h(x) ) ψ : a planar map that magnifies wall interiors (registered, from the earlier speed-equalization study)
 h : the lift, unchanged, from the clearance in the maze
ψ gives a wall two sides that are far apart; h gives the band its length along the normal.
The loss and its slopes are still taken in x (chain rule through ψ and h); the push is unchanged. 
The map is not new. It is the speed-equalization map with unconstrained wall interiors (core target 0.1) from the earlier pages, produced by the same optimizer on every scene. On the plus maze it stretches wall interiors ×3.2 along the wall normal and shrinks free space to ×0.61; the band keeps its length (×0.93), which is why the map could not help the wall layer. Used alone it had the best planning and the worst travel times of all planar maps on the plus maze: 94.9 % / 11.7 % (queries solved / travel times within 10 %), with detours overestimated (index 1.62, where 1 is the truth). The lift alone was the mirror image: 57.9 % / 74.5 %, detours seen through. The two repair each other.

Control: which property of the map does the work 
A second map tests whether the distance between the two sides of a wall is what matters. It is closed-form, the gradient map of a blurred obstacle indicator, ψ(x) = x − k ∇(G σ ∗ occupancy), one-to-one by construction. It moves the two sides of a wall apart as much as the optimized map does (distance between facing points ×1.32 against ×1.32), but stretches the wall interior only ×1.5 instead of ×3.2 and leaves free space away from walls as it is. Plus maze, one seed: with the lift and no push it plans 59.5 % / 72.8 %, like the lift alone, and the field is see-through again (index 0.39). So the distance between the two sides is not what holds the detours without a push, and a modest widening still helps the push keep them. The optimized map differs from this one in two ways, the ×3 stretch of wall interiors and the compression of all free space. 
The gap maze points to the wall stretch. Its two optimized maps compress free space about equally (×0.76 and ×0.69) and differ in how much they stretch wall interiors (×1.6 and ×5.7). The two maps also differ in the band (×1.37 and ×1.08), so this is evidence, not a clean separation.

The alternative without a map: finer position features 
The network’s position inputs pass through random Fourier features, and multiplying their frequencies by 3 also gives a thin wall more room. With the lift and the push: disks / floor plan / plus maze / gap maze 94.1 / 79.7 / 92.3 / 96.1 % solved and 97.6 / 59.1 / 77.9 / 89.6 % within 10 % (3 / 3 / 3 / 3 seeds). It fixes the plus maze (92.3 % / 77.9 %) and costs the floor plan (79.7 % / 59.1 % against 94.2 % / 82.1 %), where the see-through fields come back, and a few points on the disks. Controls on the plus maze: the same features and push without the lift 70.2 % / 58.0 %; the same features and the lift without the push 62.2 % / 77.2 % (frequencies ×2); plain ntrl-demo with frequencies ×3 89.9 % / 5.3 %. It needs no map, and it is not safe on every scene, so it is not part of the recipe. 
 What this says about the original idea. A map of the plane does have a job in learning the travel-time field, but it is not raising the slowest speed. The speed problem sits along the wall normal, and the lift solves it exactly without a map. What a planar map can add is room inside the obstacles, so that the two sides of a wall are far apart for the network. The max-min map spent its room on neither.

The recipe in a dozen lines 
Steps 1 and 2 use only what an eikonal learner already has: the clearance function behind its speed labels, and a collision check. Step 0 needs a map of the scene, here the registered one from the speed-equalization study. 
# 0. (where walls are thin against the detours they cause) a planar map ψ that magnifies wall interiors;
# replace x by ψ(x) in the network input below. Everything else, h included, stays in the maze's coordinates.
# 1. the lift: one more input coordinate, from the clearance the speed labels already use
d, n = clearance(x) # signed distance to the obstacles and its unit gradient
h = G(d) # G(d) = ∫_d^margin √(1 / S(d')² − 1) dd' (a 1-D table; 0 in free space;
 # continued with the floor slope as deep as the training points go)
T = net(fourier([x0, h0]), fourier([x1, h1]))
 # ∂h/∂x = G'(d) n, so ∇_x T follows by the chain rule
# 2. the selection term, added to the eikonal loss (faded in; λ strong first, then relaxed)
blocked = segment_hits_obstacle(x0, x1) & (T < 3 box lengths) # a collision check, no solver; bounded
loss += − λ · mean(T[blocked]) + μ · mean(relu(√(S |∇_x T|) − 1)²) 
Limits and open points 
 The lift needs the clearance at query time , the same function the training speeds come from. ntrl-demo evaluates only the network when it plans; a lifted field also evaluates d(x). 
 Lift and push alone do not hold long detours behind thin walls. The lift gives the band length along the normal; the difference between the two sides of a wall still has to be built inside the wall’s own width in the plane. On the 22 × 22 plus maze the recipe’s fields know, of a true detour of 2.3, 4.3, 8.0, 14.1 cells (medians by length class), 1.2, 2.4, 2.4, 1.6 cells. A wall-magnifying planar map in front of the lift closes this (99.8 % solved, Part 3). That map is the open point now: it comes from a lattice optimizer for 2-D scenes, minutes per scene, with no 3-D or configuration-space version. The closed-form map tried here recovers about half of the gain, and only with the push. Which property of the optimized map carries the result is not cleanly separated; the gap maze points to the stretch of wall interiors (Part 3). 
 The push trades accuracy for planning, and the schedule only partly escapes the trade. Constant push, one seed, queries solved / travel times within 10 %: λ 3e-3: plus maze 66.9 % / 88.7 %, gap maze 84.6 % / 92.7 %; λ 6e-3: plus maze 68.2 % / 51.5 %, gap maze 93.4 % / 58.3 %; λ 1e-2: plus maze 79.0 % / 12.2 %, gap maze 92.3 % / 24.1 %. On the gap maze strong-then-relaxed keeps the planning of the strong push and the accuracy of the weak one; on the plus maze it ends where the weak push does. The balance between push and one-sided term was set by one argument and one seed, not tuned per scene. 
 The push must be bounded wherever free space is not connected (the floor plan’s outside), and it has to be faded in: switched on at once it hangs ntrl-demo’s trainer (its epoch-repeat rule). A push on every pair with the one-sided term deflates the field instead of selecting anything. Fading the lift into a trained plain network does not keep its detours. These first attempts are in the run table. 
 Not tested: 3-D scenes, configuration spaces, other speed profiles, more than one seed on the two larger mazes. In a configuration space the lift’s normal speed is 1 / |∇ q d| instead of 1.
