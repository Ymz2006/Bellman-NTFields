A coordinate of the pair does not fix the lift. The lifted length of a short step, used as its cost, does.

Two tasks were asked for: give the lifted network the length of the lifted curve of the straight segment between the two points (Task 1), or simply a −1 / +1 flag when that segment is blocked (Task 2). As a network input, neither helps on the 22 × 22 plus maze: 59.2 % and 58.2 % of the queries solved against 68.2 % for lift + push and 65.0 % for ntrl-demo, with the detours still seen through. The lifted network turns out to be able to hold the step across a wall once it is shown the true travel times, so what is missing is not an input but a loss that selects the right field. The same quantity, the lifted length of a straight segment, supplies that loss when it is used as the cost of a short step in a Bellman backup: 98.4 % of the queries solved over 3 training seeds (97.6 / 100.0 / 97.5), 83.3 % of the travel times within 10 % of the truth, 0.1 % of the detours seen through, with no push and no pair input. With candidate steps up to 5.3 cells long: 97.4 % (93.1 / 99.2 / 100.0), and detours 57 % too long in excess. On larger scenes the backup needs its weight scaled with the step and the longer steps; with both, one training seed per scene: plus 30 81.5 % (ntrl-demo 56.8, lift + push 64.2, with octaves 76.2); plus 40 71.5 % (ntrl-demo 59.2, lift + push 63.2, with octaves 68.8); Allensville 43 100.0 % (ntrl-demo 90.3, lift + push 99.6, with octaves 99.9); apartment 64 77.6 % with steps up to 0.24 box lengths (ntrl-demo 66.3, lift + push 73.0, with octaves 72.8); Lakeville 83 97.9 % (ntrl-demo 69.1, lift + push 76.5, with octaves 97.9); Klickitat 96 100.0 % with steps up to 0.24 box lengths (ntrl-demo 71.2, lift + push 85.5, with octaves 98.7). A second request (Part 6): a lift trained with pairs inside obstacles has the right speed everywhere without the right travel time. With only that model’s speed in the backup, as the cost of a step and as the collision check, 97.8 % of the queries are solved (98.8 / 95.3 / 99.4); with the backup added to that model’s own training, 88.8 % (80.0 / 98.3 / 88.2).

4. Why do the pair inputs fail?
Three measured reasons. The loss learns to ignore them. The input jumps wherever the straight segment starts to cross an obstacle, in open space, where the true travel time is continuous; the term that keeps the field continuous there removes the dependence on the input within 100 epochs (zeroing the pair column of the trained flag network changes its travel times by a median 0.33 cells), and the push then lifts blocked and clear pairs together. A flag does not separate the two faces of a wall when both are blocked from the goal, which is the usual case in a maze. An input that does separate them (a count, an unsaturated length) puts cliffs in open space: fitted to the truth, such fields change by more than twice the step on 63 % of the 0.1-cell steps across a line where the count changes (0.4 % for the lift alone) and plan 63.5 % with 99.4 % of their travel times within 10 %.
5. What makes the idea work?
yesUsing the lifted length where it is exact: over a short straight step it is the step’s travel time, and it is very large when the step crosses a wall. A Bellman backup over short free steps, T(q, g) = min over steps of [step cost + T(step end, g)], raises exactly the configurations a see-through field gets wrong. Lift + backup on the 22-cell maze: 98.4 % solved, 83.3 % of travel times within 10 %, 0.1 % seen through; a true step of 8.2 cells across a wall is held as 9.6. With steps up to 5.3 cells: 97.4 % solved, 63.1 % of travel times within 10 %.

Part 1 · Can the lifted network hold a step across a wall at all?

This is the premise of both tasks, so it was tested directly. Each network was regressed on the true travel time (the grid solver’s, 48 goals, 600,000 pairs) and then asked two things: how much of the true step in travel time it holds between mirror configurations on the two faces of a wall, and how it plans.

After 6,000 steps every network holds about a third of the step (0.30 ntrl-demo, 0.29 lift, 0.33 lift + wall count) while 96.6 % of the lift’s travel times are already within 10 %: the bulk of the field is learned first and the two faces of the walls last. Trained longer, with most samples next to walls, the plain lifted network holds 0.95 of the step and plans 83.9 % (0.94 and 86.8 % with uniform samples and 30,000 steps). ntrl-demo without the lift also holds the step (0.94) but plans 58.8 %, with 33.9 % of its plans through a wall: the lift is what makes the band next to the wall learnable.

So on this maze, with walls 0.3 cells thick in configuration space, the two faces of a wall are not too close for the network. They are too close for the losses used so far to tell apart. The pair inputs do not make the step easier to fit either: at 6,000 steps they hold 0.29–0.33 of it, and at 20,000 the flag is level with the lift (0.97, 85.3 % solved) while the count and the unsaturated length plan far worse (63.5 %, 63.2 %, 61.8 %) although 99.4 % of their travel times are within 10 %.

The reason is in the chart above. A pair input that tells the two faces of a wall apart also changes in open space, wherever the straight segment to the goal starts to cross one more obstacle. On 5.8 % of 0.1-cell steps in open space the wall count changes. The truth is continuous there. The count-fed field is not: its travel time changes by a median 2.8 times the step across such a line (90th percentile 8.3), and a planner that follows the field stops at those cliffs (34.4 % of its plans stall). The flag has few such cliffs (2.1 %) because the fit learned to ignore it.

The lifted length appended to the embedding: 2, 8, 32, 128 copies

The first form in the request. ψ = tanh of the up-and-down part of the lifted curve of the straight segment (cells; 0 on a clear segment along which the lift is monotone, saturating at one wall), −ψ / 2 at one end and +ψ / 2 at the other, appended k times to the 256-wide embedding, either before the instance norm or after it. After the norm the metric reads the copies directly: every 16 of them add 0.2 × amplitude × ψ to the travel time (amplitude 0.3; 0.075 for 128 copies, so that 32 and 128 add the same 2.7 cells). Same test as above: fitted to the truth for 20,000 steps, then planned.

A few copies do no harm and no measurable good: 2 and 8 copies plan 86.2 % and 86.2 % before the norm, 83.8 % and 87.2 % after it, against 83.9 % for the lift alone (the fits without a pair input range from 83.9 to 86.8 %), and all hold 0.95–0.99 of the step. Many copies hurt: 32 copies 77.2 % before the norm and 54.3 % after it, 128 copies 40.8 % and 53.2 %, with 58.5 % and 46.8 % of the plans stalled and travel times less accurate than the lift’s own (90.3 % and 92.8 % within 10 % against 99.8 %). The more of the embedding the feature occupies, the more the travel time follows it, and it is a function of the straight segment: it jumps in open space where the segment starts to cross a wall, and it says the same thing for a wall with a door next to it and a wall with a 20-cell detour.

Part 2 · The pair feature as a network input, trained without the truth

The pair input can only make the right field representable. ntrl-demo’s loss accepts fields that pass straight through obstacles (lift page, Part 3), so every arm here also gets the lift page’s push on pairs whose segment is blocked. One more term is needed because the input is discontinuous: the eikonal term is a derivative at a point and cannot tie the field on the two sides of a line where the input jumps. The cap does: over random free steps of up to 0.66 cells, the travel time may not change by more than the step’s own travel time.

Flag alone. 40.7 % solved. From the first epoch the blocked pairs sit at a travel time of about 11 box lengths: the −1 / +1 column shifts the phases of all 128 Fourier rows, so the two ends of a blocked pair start out as unrelated points. ntrl-demo’s loss weights every pair by exp(−T / 2), so at that travel time the pair no longer counts, and nothing corrects it. Flag + push: the same, 53.8 %.

Flag + push + cap. 59.2 % solved, travel times fine (81.0 % within 10 %), detours seen through (91 %). The cap brings the blocked pairs down in the first 100 epochs, and it does so by making the network insensitive to the flag: with the pair column zeroed the trained field’s travel times change by a median 0.51 cells at epoch 100, 0.42 at 2,000 and 0.33 at the end. The push then has no handle that separates blocked from clear pairs: at epoch 2,000 clear pairs are 7 % long and the detours are still seen through. Even pairs blocked by a single obstacle, where a flag is all the information needed, stay seen through (89 %, detour index 0.20).

The lifted length, in the ways the request lists. Saturated (tanh of the up-and-down part of the lifted curve, which is 0 on clear segments): 58.2 %. Wall count: 56.8 %. One more thing was tried and dropped: with the derivative of the length feature inside the eikonal term (so that the loss sees the total derivative of the field the planner reads) the loss diverges within 100 epochs, because the feature’s gradient reaches 1,300 per box length where a segment grazes a wall. The length arms above leave that derivative out.

The step across a wall, which the pair input was meant to supply: with the flag, a true step of 8.2 cells is held as 0.7, and the travel time between the two faces themselves is 1.5 cells (lift alone 0.8 and 2.1).

Part 3 · The lifted length as the cost of a short step

The lifted curve of a straight segment has an exact meaning only when the segment is short: its length is then the travel time of the step (along a wall normal the two are equal by construction of the lift), and a step that crosses a wall is longer than any detour worth taking. That is a local cost, and a local cost with a minimum over steps is the Bellman equation of the travel time. The term added to ntrl-demo’s loss:

for each end q of a training pair (q, g): 16 straight steps q → q′ of 0.33–0.66 cells that stay out of obstacles (2 down the field’s own gradient, 6 around it, 8 in random directions) y = min over the steps of [ τ(q, q′) + T(q′, g) ] τ = travel time of the step = ∫ ds / S along it; no gradient through y loss += mean (T(q, g) − y)² and ntrl-demo’s own temporal-difference step is dropped where it crosses an obstacle

A field that descends into a wall has no free step that lowers it there, so y is above T and the term raises it, at that configuration only; the correction then travels back along the detour one step per backup. No pair is pushed and nothing is asked of pairs that are already consistent. ntrl-demo has a term of this kind, a 0.66-cell step down the gradient, but takes the step without a collision check: at the start of training 38 % of those steps cross an obstacle and 25 % at the end, each one equating the travel time on one face of a wall with the travel time on the other.

Lift + backup: 98.4 % solved (97.6 / 100.0 / 97.5 over 3 training seeds), 0.9 % of the plans through a wall and 0.7 % stalled; 83.3 % of travel times within 10 % (median learned / true 1.032), line-of-sight pairs 91.0 %, 0.1 % of the detours seen through. The detours are 18 % long in excess (detour index 1.18). Queries with a true path of 32–64 cells, which no earlier lift arm solved without an inflated field: 95 %.

Dropping the wall-crossing TD steps without adding the backup gives 49.3 % and a field 38 % short: ntrl-demo’s step was also what kept the far field’s scale. The backup without the lift (first version): 72.6 % with 24.6 % of the plans through a wall, so the lift still does its own job, the band next to the wall. Keeping ntrl-demo’s wall-crossing TD steps next to the backup: 98.5 %. Weight 3: 85.7 %. Half the step radius: 79.3 %. Longer steps (lengths log-uniform between 0.33 and 5.3 cells): 97.4 % solved (93.1 / 99.2 / 100.0), no detour seen through from epoch 600 on, but detours 57 % too long in excess and 63.1 % of travel times within 10 %: a long step carries the correction farther per update, and the minimum over 16 sampled steps overestimates more when the steps are long. A pair input on top of the working recipe does not help: flag 78.5 %, wall count on the first version 61.2 % against 96.8 %.

Across a wall (chart in Part 2): a true step of 4.0 cells is held as 4.4, 8.2 as 9.6, 13.5 as 16.0, and the travel time between the two faces themselves, 0.8 cells apart, is 19.2 cells where the lift alone says 2.1. The network and its input are the lift’s, unchanged. The embeddings of the two faces are far apart because the loss asked for it.

The first version ran 28 % long, and why

The first two backup runs planned 96.8 % and 95.6 % but with every travel time about 28 % too long (6.5 % within 10 %), at weight 1 and at weight 0.1 alike. The cause was the step’s travel time: a 6-point trapezoid rule on 1 / S. In the band 1 / S falls from 83 to 9 within 0.0175 cells of the wall, so a step that starts there is overestimated: 1.09 times the reference on average and 2.5 times at the 99th percentile. On the trained field the band configurations were 0.04 cells below their targets and the open-space ones 0.14 cells above: an equilibrium between an inflated target at the walls and the correct one elsewhere. The step time now uses the antiderivative of 1 / S between samples (0.997 of the reference on average, 1.004 at the 99th percentile). The inflated fields plan as well as the correct one, as over-priced walls did on the earlier pages; only the correct one has travel times that can be used.

How far is the backup from what the network can do?

The representation test of Part 1, on the two scenes where the backup falls short. Regressed on the true travel times, the lifted network plans 70.5 % on the 40-cell maze (74.9 % with the octaves), with 99.5 % of its travel times within 10 % and no query of 64–128 cells solved. The backup field plans 71.5 % there. So on the 40-cell maze the backup plans as well as a field that was shown the right travel times, and a better loss alone would not go further. The plans that fail stop in front of a wall they have to go around (28.2 % stalled, 0.2 % through a wall), and the fitted field holds only 0.55 of the true step across walls (0.94 with the octaves) after the 20,000 steps that gave 0.95 on the 22-cell maze: here the step across a wall is the part the plain lifted network is slow to fit, and the octaves help. Part 5 · Training pairs inside obstacles

Every run above trains on pairs with both ends in free space. In the lift-scale study this variant did not make the walls felt (22-cell maze: lift 63.3 % solved, lift + push 65.3 %; apartment: lift 63.6 %). With the backup as it is, obstacle samples break the recipe. 22-cell maze: 59.4 % solved against 98.4 % on free-space pairs (three training seeds, 97.6 / 100.0 / 97.5), 71 % of the detours seen through, and even the travel times of pairs in line of sight are within 10 % for only 70 % (91 %). Part 6 · A model with the right speed and the wrong travel time

The request: the “lift, flat top, whole interior” model of the Lift Variants page samples pairs inside obstacles, so it recovers the speed there; it satisfies the eikonal equation without being the viscosity solution. Two uses were asked for: (1) keep only its speed and integrate it along a candidate step as the backup’s step cost, in place of the lifted length; (2) add the backup to that model’s own training, skipped where a pair has an end inside an obstacle. Everything here is the 22-cell maze, training seed 87358, one training per arm unless a range is given.

Rebuilding the model on the local setup

The page’s code is not on this machine and its robot was a point in 2-D; here it is the rectangle in SE(2) of every local study. Three lifts trained alone, with no push and no backup: flat top, 20 %: the lift that stops 0.0175 cells into an obstacle, on pairs whose two ends may lie anywhere inside one, thinned so that 20 % of the pairs have an end inside (the share of the original); flat top, 68 %: the same sampler unthinned (the rectangle’s obstacles are 0.3–0.6 cells thick, so most first ends land inside); lift into the walls, 20 %: the lift continued 0.3 cells into the walls (the page’s “ridge”), on the lift-scale study’s pairs with the second end of 20 % inside an obstacle.

The page’s result is not reproduced by the flat top at 20 %. It solves 58.1 % and sees through 98.5 % of the detours, the level of the lift on free-space pairs (55.0 %, 98.3 %); the page reported 65.5 % and 18 % on its plus maze. The reason is in the speed: inside obstacles its S |∇T| is 0.23 (1 = exact; 0.01 with no pair inside, 0.80 on the page), so a wall costs 0.35 of its true crossing time. With 68 % of the pairs inside it reaches 0.58 and sees through 60 %, and pays as the page describes: travel times 12 % long, the predicted speed 11 % too slow in open space. The model with the right speed everywhere is the lift continued into the walls: S |∇T| 1.01 inside obstacles, 0.99 in the band, 1.00 in open space, a step through a wall at 0.97 of its true cost. It is the model of the request: the eikonal equation holds inside and outside the walls, and it is not the viscosity solution (63.3 % solved, 54 % of the detours seen through, travel times within 10 % for 81 % of the pairs).

Idea 1 · Only its speed, as the cost of a step in the backup

The frozen model’s predicted speed 1 / |∇T| is tabulated on the grid of the clearance table (32 nodes per cell, 49 headings, the log of the slowness averaged over 4 random partners). The backup of Part 3 is unchanged except for the cost of a candidate step: its length times the mean of the tabulated slowness along it, in place of the integral of the true speed (the lifted length). The network is trained on free-space pairs, as in Part 3. Steps through obstacles are handled in one of two ways, neither of which reads the scene: no check (a step through a wall is a candidate and costs what the speed says) or a check from the speed (a step is dropped when the tabulated speed falls below a threshold at a sample).

With the speed of the lift-into-walls model and the check from that speed: 97.8 % solved, 0.6 % of the detours seen through, travel times within 10 % for 74 % of the pairs, against 98.4 % (97.6 / 100.0 / 97.5), 0.1 % and 83 % for the true speed with the geometric collision check. Per training seed: 98.8 / 95.3 / 99.4 %. The weakest seed loses 4.7 % of its plans, every one a plan that crosses a wall on a 32–64-cell query (on planning seed 0: 14 plans, 0.15–0.27 cells deep; 0.15 cells is half a wall when the robot travels along it). The true-speed runs lose plans the same way on the same queries (0.8 / 1.9 / 0.0 % of their plans; on planning seed 0, 3 and 5 plans). The likely reading: the field is right on both sides of a thin wall and ntrl-demo’s planner, which does not test collisions, steps across. The check itself has no gap: of 591,800 wall crossings sampled against the table that seed read, none passes at any threshold from 0.04 to 0.2. Without any check: 80.4 % solved (0 % of the 32–64-cell queries). The control with the true speed and no check: 99.2 % solved, 100 % of the 32–64-cell queries, 0.0 % seen through. With the flat-top 68 % model’s speed and no check: 83.3 % solved and 0.8 % seen through; every query with a true path up to 32 cells is solved (600 of 600) and none of the 120 longer ones; travel times are 27 % long, which is that model’s speed error in open space carried into the field.

What the step cost has to get right. With the true speed the backup needs no collision check on this maze: 99.2 % solved, every 32–64-cell query, 0.0 % of the detours seen through. At ntrl-demo’s floor speed (0.012) an obstacle is a very slow strip: crossing the thinnest face-on section of a wall (0.3 cells for the rectangle) costs 25 cells of travel time, more than a crossing saves anywhere in this maze. The margin is thin. The lift-into-walls model prices a wall crossing at 0.84–0.97 of its true cost (5 % and 50 % quantiles over 54,687 crossing steps), the flat-top 68 % model at 0.40–0.71. With the lift-into-walls model’s speed and no check the field solved 96.2 % at epoch 3,000 on planning seed 0 and ended at 80.4 %: at the end the detours of 12–24 cells are 24 % seen through and 0 % of the 32–64-cell queries are solved. The table is the only difference to the true-speed run, and taking the crossing steps out of the candidates (next) restores the result, so the crossings are the cause: priced 3–16 % low, they open the longest detours. With the check made from the same speed the walls are hard again and the under-pricing no longer matters: 97.8 % solved, 99 % of the 32–64-cell queries. What the model’s speed costs is accuracy of the travel times: 6 % long at the median, within 10 % for 74 % of the pairs (83 % with the true speed).

In these runs ntrl-demo’s own TD term still skips steps that cross an obstacle by the geometric test, as in Part 3. With that term left as ntrl-demo has it, so that no loss term tests a step against the scene: 99.6 % solved, 0.7 % seen through, travel times within 10 % for 89 % of the pairs (one seed). The lift and the speed labels come from the scene as in every run of this page.

Idea 1 on the 40-cell maze

The large-scene recipe of Part 4 (lift + octaves + backup with candidate steps up to 5.3 cells), with the step cost and the check taken from the speed of that maze’s own lift-into-walls model (with octaves): 88.6 % solved (97.0 / 80.1), 0.3 % of the detours seen through, travel times within 10 % for 66 % of the pairs, against 86.8 % (81.5 / 92.1), 0.0 % and 55 % with the true speed and the geometric check. The same class separated the two true-speed variants of Part 4 (0 % against 72 %), so on this maze a single training does not rank two methods.

Idea 2 · The backup added to the model, skipped inside obstacles

The model with the right speed (lift into the walls, 20 % of the pairs with an end inside) trained with the backup of Part 3, the backup applied only to pairs with both ends out of obstacles: 88.8 % solved over 3 training seeds (80.0 / 98.3 / 88.2), 0.8 % of the detours seen through, against 63.3 % and 54 % for the model alone. The backup removes nearly all of the see-through in every seed (2.2 / 0.2 / 0.1 %). What varies is the longest class of queries: 0 / 100 / 31 % of the 32–64-cell queries solved, against 96–100 % of all the shorter ones. On seed 87358 the field solved 98.3 % at epoch 3,000 on planning seed 0 and lost the longest queries in the last 2,000 epochs; where those plans stall, the learned travel time is 0.78–0.85 of the truth, so the far field is short again there. The same backup on free-space pairs holds them in all three seeds (97.6 / 100.0 / 97.5 %), and idea 1 keeps the pairs inside obstacles out of the second training altogether. With the backup applied to every pair and the geometric check it fails (59.4 %, Part 5).

The backup on every pair, with no collision check: a step through an obstacle is a candidate and costs its floor-speed travel time, so the backup is the Bellman equation of the same passable-wall problem whose eikonal equation the pairs inside obstacles train, and a goal inside a wall is reached by steps into it. 67.4 % solved, 15.0 % seen through, travel times within 10 % for 64 % of the pairs, 0 % of the 32–64-cell queries. That is worse than skipping the pairs inside obstacles, and it did not improve during training (60–61 % on planning seed 0 at epochs 1,000, 2,000 and 3,000). No control was run on why.

The flat-top lift on the 20 % whole-interior pairs with the same backup: 68.2 % solved, 18.9 % seen through (58.1 % and 98.5 % without the backup). Started from the trained model instead of from scratch: 87.8 % solved, 6.5 % seen through.

What this does not show

The pair input was tested on the rectangle robot in SE(2), not on the point robot of the lift pages; the three failure mechanisms do not depend on the robot, but the numbers are this setup’s. The pair inputs were trained with the push and the cap; a pair input under some other selection term was tested only once, on top of the backup. The backup needs a collision check of short straight steps and the clearance along them, the same information the lift and the push already use; its weight, number of candidate steps and radius were chosen on the 22-cell maze, where a three times larger weight or half the radius lose 12–18 points, all on the longest queries. The backup’s step time reads the clearance from a table (32 nodes per cell, within 1 % of the exact step time on average); the run with the exact rectangle clearance was stopped at epoch 2,400 to free the GPU and is not a result.
