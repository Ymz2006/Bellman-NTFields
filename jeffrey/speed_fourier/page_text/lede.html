You asked whether Fourier embeddings work better when they capture the speed across the environment. They capture it easily, but planning does not improve. A sum of sin/cos along a line fits the speed with the 20–30 terms you guessed. Yet on the 22×22 maze, 16 such rows take planning from {{zero22}} % down to {{true22}} %, and handing the network the exact speed takes it to {{oracle22}} %.

The likely reason is that speed reads the same on both sides of a wall. The network's T is a distance between two embeddings, so two points are far apart only if their embeddings differ, and speed gives them no difference. Rows that do differ across walls, corridor distance to 16 landmarks, reach {{geo22}} %, the best result in this study on that maze, though they use the exact maze graph.

A follow-up replaced speed with its integral along the line, which is the travel time along that line. That removes the damage: the exact integral reaches {{p2_exact22}} %. It still does not beat the {{p2_oct22}} % of the plain octaves, because a straight-line integral goes through walls and so does not tell the two sides apart either.

A second follow-up dropped the octaves and used the integral embedding alone. It then plans at the level of ntrl-demo's random rows: {{p3_exact13}} % on 13×13 and {{p3_exact22}} % on 22×22, against {{p3_oct13}} % and {{p3_oct22}} % for the octaves on the same seed.

A third follow-up tried step functions and norms next to the octaves. Counting the walls a ray hits ahead does not help. Putting an L1 norm on the integral does, in one place: 16 landmark rows, each the L1 norm over directions of the integral differences to a landmark cell, reach {{p4_mark22}} % on 22×22, {{p4d_markzero22}} points over empty rows and above the octaves alone.
