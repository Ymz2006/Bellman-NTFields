Test 1: ray hit counts

Sixteen rays, 22.5° apart, counted to the edge of the maze. Each row is a step function: it drops by one when the point passes a wall on its ray. Planning moves by {{p4s_hitszero22}} points against empty rows, inside the spread between seeds. The rows with the walls flipped do no worse (true minus flipped: {{p4s_hitshitsflip22}} points), so the placement of the steps carries nothing the network uses. The screen pointed the same way: its score is {{p4scr_hits22}}. Moving one cell changes the counts about as much through an open passage as across a wall, because the rays in the other directions gain and lose walls either way.

Test 2: where the norm can go

I listed five places and screened them before training.

  • In the distance. T gets an added term, the L1 or L∞ norm of the difference between the two endpoints' integral rows, with a weight. When the weight is trained it shrinks from 1 to {{p4hw_headl122}} for L1 and {{p4hw_headlinf22}} for L∞, and planning stays near the integral rows alone ({{p4ok_headl122}} and {{p4ok_headlinf22}} of 720, against {{p4first_exact22}} for the rows alone on the same seed). When the weight is fixed at 0.2 the term cannot be removed, and planning falls to {{p4_headl1f22}} % and {{p4_headlinff22}} %.
  • Against landmarks. Sixteen landmark cells spread over the maze. Row m is the norm, over 64 directions, of the difference between the point's integrals and the landmark's. With the L1 norm this is the arm that works. With the L∞ norm it reaches {{p4_markinf22}} %, {{p4s_markinfzero22}} points against empty rows.
  • Across directions at a point. The 16 integral rows are divided by their own L1 or L∞ norm. One seed each: {{p4_intnl122}} % and {{p4_intnlinf22}} %, against {{p4o_intnl1exact22}} % for the plain integral rows on that seed. Not separable from seed noise.
  • Along the ray. The L∞ norm of 1 / speed along a ray is the largest slowness on it, which is the same for nearly every ray that meets a wall. That collapses to test 1, so it was not trained separately.
  • On the network weights. An L1 penalty on the first layer would change ntrl-demo's training loop, so I left it out.

Why the landmark rows work

Two controls isolate the walls. The same landmark rows built with speed 1 everywhere hold no walls and reach {{p4_markunit22}} %. The same rows read at (−x, −y) hold the walls in the wrong place and reach {{p4_markflip22}} %. The true rows beat them by {{p4s_markmarkunit22}} and {{p4s_markmarkflip22}} points, on every seed.

The rows tell the two sides of a wall apart before any training: their screen score is {{p4scr_mark22}}, against {{p4scr_exact22}} for the 16 integral rows they are built from. The L1 norm sums the differences in all 64 directions, so a wall that changes many of them moves the row. The L∞ norm keeps only the single largest direction, and its screen score is {{p4scr_markinf22}}. That ordering matches the planning results, but I did not test the cause separately.

The trained fields agree. With the landmark rows the difference across a wall is {{p4jump_mark22}} of the true one, against {{p4jump_zero22}} with empty rows and {{p4jump_geo22}} with corridor-distance rows. The network reads the rows when it plans: holding them constant removes {{p4fz_mark22}} % of the successes, against {{p4fz_geo22}} % for the corridor-distance rows, {{p4fz_markunit22}} % and {{p4fz_markflip22}} % for the two controls, and {{p4fz_hits22}} % for the hit counts. The L∞ landmark rows are barely used: holding them constant removes {{p4fz_markinf22}} %.

Landmark rows built from hit counts instead of integrals reach {{p4_markhits22}} %, {{p4s_markhitszero22}} points against empty rows. Their flipped control reaches {{p4_markhitsflip22}} %, and true minus flipped is {{p4s_markhitsmarkhitsflip22}} points, so a gain from the walls is not established for them. Their fields are rougher: {{p4bumps_markhits22}} local minima per path against {{p4bumps_mark22}}.

Why the norm in the distance fails

The network's T is zero or more, so an added term can only be corrected downwards by shrinking its weight. At weight 1 the L1 term is already larger than the true travel time on {{p4above_l1one22}} % of the queries and the L∞ term on {{p4above_linfone22}} %, because a straight-line integral crosses walls that the true path goes around. That is the likely reason the trained weight goes to zero. ntrl-demo's weight decay also pulls the weight down, to about 0.002 on its own, so the trained-weight runs alone do not prove that training rejects the term. The fixed-weight runs settle it. With the weight fixed at 0.2 the term exceeds the true time on only {{p4above_l1fifth22}} % and {{p4above_linffifth22}} % of the queries, and it still breaks planning: T gets {{p4bumps_headl1f22}} local minima per path for L1 and {{p4bumps_headlinff22}} for L∞, against {{p4bumps_exact22}} without the term, and only {{p4conv_headl1f22}} % and {{p4conv_headlinff22}} % of the plans reach the goal, against {{p4conv_exact22}} % without it. A straight-line integral jumps when its line moves past a wall end. Those jumps become local minima of T, and the planner stops in them.
